# Tidal Resource Physics & Methodology

## 1. Why Tidal Currents Exist

### 1.1 Astronomical Forcing

Tides are long-period surface gravity waves generated by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun. The dominant period is the **semi-diurnal lunar tide (M₂)** at 12.42 hours — the time between successive passes of the Moon over a given meridian. Because the tidal wave is forced at ocean-basin scale, it propagates as a shallow-water wave; even in the deepest ocean (~5 km), λ >> h, so the entire water column moves in phase.

### 1.2 Tidal Constituents

Real tides are a superposition of harmonics, each with a frequency locked to an astronomical period:

| Constituent | Origin | Period | Typical Amplitude (Philippines) |
|-------------|--------|--------|----------------------------------|
| M₂ | Principal lunar semi-diurnal | 12.42 h | 0.3–1.0 m |
| S₂ | Principal solar semi-diurnal | 12.00 h | 0.1–0.3 m |
| K₁ | Luni-solar diurnal | 23.93 h | 0.2–0.5 m |
| O₁ | Principal lunar diurnal | 25.82 h | 0.1–0.3 m |

These four constituents account for ~80–90% of tidal variability. The model reconstructs the free-surface at each open boundary from them:

$$
\eta(t) = \sum_{k} A_k(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \cos\bigl(\omega_k t + \phi_k(\mathbf{x})\bigr)
$$

### 1.3 Spring–Neap Cycle

M₂ and S₂ beat together every 14.77 days (the synodic period):

- **Spring tide:** M₂ and S₂ in phase → maximum tidal range → highest currents
- **Neap tide:** M₂ and S₂ out of phase → minimum tidal range → lowest currents

This is why the simulation runs for at least 15 days: a 14.77-day spring–neap cycle is the shortest window that captures the full velocity envelope. Any shorter window risks sampling only springs (over-estimating the resource) or only neaps (under-estimating it).

## 2. From Tidal Elevation to Current Velocity

The tide gauge tells you water level — but a tidal stream turbine extracts kinetic energy from the **current**. So how does a 1-metre surface slope translate into a multi-metre-per-second current? That is the job of the shallow-water equations.

### 2.1 The Shallow-Water Equations (SWE)

The SWE are derived from the Navier-Stokes equations by depth-averaging and assuming hydrostatic pressure. They are the standard model for tides because horizontal scales (10–1000 km) vastly exceed vertical scales (10–1000 m).

#### Continuity (mass)

$$
\frac{\partial \eta}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(h u) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(h v) = 0
$$

If water converges into a cell (negative divergence), the surface rises. Straightforward volume conservation.

#### Momentum (Newton)

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \underbrace{u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}}_{\text{advection}}
\;-\!\! \underbrace{f v}_{\text{Coriolis}}
\;=\;
-\!\!\underbrace{g \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial x}}_{\text{pressure gradient}}
\;-\!\! \underbrace{\frac{\tau_{b,x}}{\rho h}}_{\text{bottom friction}}
\;+\!\! \underbrace{A_h \nabla^2 u}_{\text{turbulent mixing}}
$$

Each term has a physical job:

| Term | Physics | When it matters |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| **Pressure gradient** | Water accelerates downhill — from high η to low η. The gravitational restoring force. | Always. This is the primary driver. |
| **Advection** | Momentum carried by the flow itself. Non-linear. | Important in constrictions where velocity is high (straits, narrow channels). |
| **Coriolis** | Earth's rotation deflects flow to the right (NH). | Relevant at scales > 10 km. Explains why tidal currents in wide channels have a cross-channel slope. |
| **Bottom friction** | Shear stress at the seabed extracts kinetic energy. Scales with u² / h. | Dominant in shallow water and channels. This is the energy sink that balances the pressure-gradient input at steady state. |
| **Horizontal mixing** | Sub-grid turbulence parameterised as a diffusive flux. | Stabilises the numerics; secondary physical role in screening models. |

### 2.2 The Balance That Sets Tidal Current Speed

In a simple tidal channel, the dominant balance is:

```
pressure gradient  ≈  bottom friction + local acceleration
```

Ignoring advection, Coriolis, and mixing, and assuming a sinusoidal along-channel surface slope of amplitude S₀ at frequency ω, the linearised momentum equation is:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -g S_0 \cos(\omega t) - \frac{C_d}{h} |u| u
$$

The quadratic drag term makes this non-linear, but an approximate solution (Lorentz linearisation) gives the **velocity amplitude**:

$$
U_{\text{max}} \;\propto\; \frac{g\,S_0\,h}{C_d}
$$

This reveals why certain sites are hotspots:

| Factor | Effect on velocity | Geographic implication |
|--------|--------------------|------------------------|
| Surface slope S₀ | Larger tidal range → larger S₀ → faster flow | Channels connecting basins with large phase difference (e.g. Pacific vs. Sulu Sea) |
| Depth h | Deeper → less friction per unit volume → faster flow | Deep straits (San Bernardino: ~200 m) are better than shallow passes |
| Drag C_d | Lower roughness → less energy loss → faster flow | Sedimentary seabeds favour higher currents than rocky/reef bottoms |
| Channel width | Mass flux conservation: Q = A·U, narrow area → higher U | The funnel effect — why straits concentrate flow |

## 3. Power Density: The ½ρU³ Law

### 3.1 Kinetic Energy Flux

A tidal stream turbine is a kinetic-energy converter. The instantaneous **power per unit swept area** is the kinetic energy flux through that area:

$$
P = \frac{1}{2} \rho \, U^3 \quad [\text{W/m}^2]
$$

where ρ = 1025 kg/m³ (seawater) and U = √(u² + v²) is the depth-averaged current speed.

### 3.2 Where the Cubic Comes From

1. Kinetic energy per unit volume: **½ ρ U²**
2. Volume flux per unit area per unit time: **U** (metres per second = m³/s per m²)
3. Product: **½ ρ U² × U = ½ ρ U³**

The cubic dependence is the defining feature of tidal stream resource assessment: **doubling the current speed gives eight times the power**. A site with 2.5 m/s is not 25% better than a 2.0 m/s site — it yields nearly double the power density. This is why accurate velocity prediction matters so much for site selection.

### 3.3 Depth-Averaging Caveat

The model computes depth-averaged velocity U_bar. Real tidal currents have a vertical profile — roughly logarithmic, with higher speeds near the surface and lower near the bed. The depth-average is:

$$
U_{\text{bar}} = \frac{1}{h} \int_{-d}^{\eta} u(z) \, dz
$$

Using U_bar³ in the power formula slightly **under-estimates** the true kinetic energy flux because ∫u³ dz > (∫u dz)³ / h². For a standard 1/7th power-law profile (u ∝ z^(1/7)), the correction factor is ~1.08 — small enough to ignore for a screening study.

## 4. Bottom Friction: The Primary Energy Sink

### 4.1 Quadratic Drag Law

The shear stress at the seabed is parameterised with a quadratic drag law:

$$
\tau_b = \rho \, C_d \, \| \mathbf{u} \| \, \mathbf{u}
$$

where C_d is the dimensionless drag coefficient, typically 0.002–0.003 for tidal flows over smooth to moderately rough seabeds. The stress enters the momentum equation as τ_b / (ρ h), giving the friction term C_d ‖u‖ u / h.

### 4.2 Depth Dependence

Notice the 1/h: **friction per unit mass is inversely proportional to water depth**. In deep water (h ~ 1000 m), friction is negligible and the tide propagates almost as a free wave. In shallow channels (h ~ 20–50 m), friction is the dominant term balancing the pressure gradient, which is why channel depth is such a strong predictor of current speed.

### 4.3 Energy Dissipation

Integrating τ_b · u over the domain gives the instantaneous power dissipated by bottom friction. Typically, 80–95% of the tidal energy entering a coastal embayment is dissipated by seabed friction — only a few percent is available for extraction before meaningfully altering the flow. This is the fundamental geophysical limit on tidal stream resource.

## 5. The Funnel Effect — Why Straits Are Hotspots

### 5.1 Mass Conservation

Consider a channel connecting two large basins with a tidal phase difference. The volume flux Q through any cross-section is:

$$
Q(t) = \iint_A u(y,z,t) \, dy \, dz
$$

If the channel narrows from width W₁ to W₂ and the flow is approximately uniform:

$$
U_2 \approx U_1 \cdot \frac{W_1}{W_2} \cdot \frac{h_1}{h_2}
$$

The current accelerates as the cross-sectional area decreases. The Philippine archipelago, with its many inter-island channels, is rich in these constriction sites.

### 5.2 Bernoulli Effect (Idealised)

For a frictionless, steady flow along a streamline:

$$
\frac{U^2}{2g} + \eta = \text{constant}
$$

A drop in free-surface across a constriction converts potential energy to kinetic energy. In the real ocean, friction and unsteadiness modify this, but the principle holds: **narrow, deep channels produce the fastest tidal streams**.

## 6. The Philippine Archipelago — Why Here?

The Philippines sits at the intersection of several tidal basins with different phase and amplitude characteristics:

- **Pacific Ocean** (east): Large M₂ amplitude, dominant incoming tide
- **South China Sea** (west): Smaller amplitude, different phase
- **Sulu Sea** (southwest): Semi-enclosed basin with resonant amplification
- **Celebes Sea** (south): Deep basin with moderate tides

The phase difference between these basins — particularly the Pacific and the Sulu Sea — drives strong flows through the inter-island straits. Known hotspots include:

| Strait | Peak Spring Current | Power Density (est.) |
|--------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| San Bernardino Strait | 3–4 m/s | > 1000 W/m² |
| Surigao Strait | 2–3 m/s | 500–1000 W/m² |
| Basilan Strait | 2–2.5 m/s | 200–500 W/m² |
| Verde Island Passage | 1.5–2 m/s | 100–300 W/m² |

## 7. From Instantaneous Velocity to a Resource Map

### 7.1 Time-Averaging

The model outputs u(t) and v(t) at every wet cell. Power density is computed at each output time-step:

$$
P(t) = \frac{1}{2} \rho \, \bigl(u(t)^2 + v(t)^2\bigr)^{3/2}
$$

Then time-averaged:

$$
\overline{P} = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T P(t) \, dt
$$

### 7.2 Why Not Just Use Peak Spring Current?

Investors and developers care about **energy yield** — total MWh per year — not just peak power. The time-average over a spring–neap cycle is a better proxy for annual energy than the instantaneous maximum. A related metric is the **P95 power density** (the power exceeded 95% of the time), which gives a conservative design basis for turbine rating.

### 7.3 Rasterisation

The unstructured or structured model grid is interpolated onto a uniform geographic grid (e.g., 500 m resolution) for visualisation and overlay with other GIS layers. Bilinear interpolation from the model's native grid preserves the spatial pattern while making the output compatible with standard GIS tooling.

## 8. Model Limitations & Screening Philosophy

### 8.1 What This Model Does Not Capture

| Omitted Physics | Impact on Resource Estimate | Direction of Bias |
|-----------------|-----------------------------|-------------------|
| 3D velocity structure | Vertical shear, turbine hub-height speed | Slight over-estimate (using depth-average) |
| Stratification (density) | Internal tides, pycnocline effects | Generally small for well-mixed channels |
| Wave-current interaction | Surface waves modify bottom stress | Small for deep channels |
| Turbine wake / array blockage | Downstream velocity deficit | Over-estimate (ignores extraction feedback) |
| Sediment transport | Morphological evolution | Not needed for screening |

### 8.2 The Screening Cascade

The philosophy is to **fail fast, fail cheap**:

```
Coarse Python model (this one)
        │
        ▼
   Sites with P_mean > 200 W/m²
        │
        ▼
   High-resolution TELEMAC-2D unstructured mesh
        │
        ▼
   Turbine-array CFD / actuator-disk model
        │
        ▼
   Geophysical / environmental surveys
        │
        ▼
   Pilot deployment
```

The screening model is **deliberately conservative**: it under-estimates current speeds (through coarse resolution and depth-averaging), so any site it flags as "Moderate" or above is almost certainly worth investigating further with more sophisticated tools.

### 8.3 Resolution vs. Accuracy

At 2 km resolution, channels narrower than ~4 km are poorly resolved, and their peak velocities will be under-estimated. This is acceptable for a first-pass screening: the model will find the large, obvious hotspots (San Bernardino, Surigao) but may miss narrow passages < 2 km wide. Those are candidates for the next tier of modelling.
